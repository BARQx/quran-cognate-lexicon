About Comparative Root Analyzer

Curious what a Qur'anic Arabic root may reveal? Enter a root and the tool turns it into its closest Hebrew cognate, then checks live Hebrew dictionaries for older or wider shades of meaning. Arabic and Hebrew are sister languages, so Hebrew can sometimes preserve a sense that is less visible in Arabic dictionaries today.

  • Start with Hebrew: See the proposed Hebrew cognate and its definitions from Sefaria and Wiktionary.
  • See the wider picture: Compare the root with reconstructed Nabataean, Syriac, Sabaic (Musnad), and Ge'ez forms, letter by letter.
  • Root classifications: Roots tagged as Weak (معتل), Assimilating (مثال), Hollow (جوف), Defective (ناقص), or Geminate (مضاعف) have flexible letters that shift or contract across Semitic languages.
  • Keep your notes: Copy a clean Markdown research summary in one click.
Please note: Comparative script forms are generated algorithmically using historical sound-change rules rather than pre-compiled word lists. If a live lookup returns empty, that reconstructed form may simply not exist in surviving texts—try searching a related root instead.

  • Reconstructed forms: A generated form is a research lead, not a claim that the word is attested in every language.

Understanding Alternate Spellings

If you see this notice, don't worry: you are looking at the real, historically verified cognate. Here is what this means in plain English:

  • Cousin languages: Arabic and Hebrew are sister languages from the same ancient Semitic family tree. Just like English "water" and German "Wasser" come from the same origin but sound slightly different, Arabic and Hebrew share roots that underwent regular sound shifts over thousands of years.
  • Why the first spelling showed no results: This tool begins by converting each Arabic letter using the standard textbook sound rule (for example, Arabic س is usually turned into Hebrew ש). But ancient languages are rich and nuanced: some words took a different path (such as ס instead of ש). If the initial formula produces a spelling that wasn't recorded in Hebrew dictionaries, the tool automatically checks documented historical variations instead of giving up.
  • What "attested" means: It means this alternate spelling is not a random guess. It is a genuine form documented in classical Hebrew and Aramaic lexicons (such as Brown-Driver-Briggs, Jastrow, or Klein).
  • Why this is helpful: Rather than leaving you with an empty result or a false dead end, the engine found the actual root used by Hebrew speakers so you can study its authentic definitions and shades of meaning.
Key Takeaway: Languages evolve over millennia. Finding an alternate spelling simply means this root took a sister branch of the phonetic family tree—and the definitions shown below reflect that authentic connection.

Understanding Root Classifications

In Arabic and sister Semitic languages (Hebrew, Aramaic, Ge'ez), most words are built around a 3-consonant foundation (like k-t-b for writing). But some roots contain flexible letters that change shape, melt into vowels, or disappear:

  • Weak Root (معتل): A root containing the flexible vowel-letters و (waw), ي (ya), or ا (alif). Because these act like vowels rather than firm consonants, they regularly stretch, transform, or drop out when words are conjugated or shift across languages.
  • Assimilating Initial (مثال / First Weak): The root begins with و or ي (e.g., وعد w-'-d). In Hebrew, an initial Arabic Waw almost always shifted to Yod (י)—for example, Arabic walad (ولد "child") corresponds to Hebrew yeled (ילד). The first letter also often vanishes in Arabic present-tense verbs (like ya'idu يَعِدُ).
  • Hollow Medial (جوف / أجوف / Second Weak): The middle consonant is و or ي (e.g., قول q-w-l or بيع b-y-'). It is called "hollow" because the middle consonant collapses into a long vowel (like qāla قال "he said" or bā'a باع "he sold") rather than holding a firm consonant sound.
  • Defective Final (ناقص / Third Weak): The root ends in و, ي, or ا (e.g., دعو d-'-w or هدي h-d-y). The final letter is called "defective" because it regularly shortens, becomes an alif maqsoora (ى), or disappears entirely in commands and jussive forms (like yad'ū يدعو becoming ud'u اُدْعُ).
  • Geminate / Doubled (مضاعف): The second and third letters are twins (e.g., مدد m-d-d or حقق h-q-q), or the root is a 2-letter base. In everyday speech and text, these identical consonants usually fuse together into a single doubled sound with a shaddah (like madda مَدَّ).
Why this matters for cognates: Because weak letters shift and drop out easily over millennia, cognates in Hebrew or Syriac often look slightly different on the surface (e.g., w-l-d vs. y-l-d). Recognizing these patterns helps connect words that share the same ancestral roots.
